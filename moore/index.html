Keeping Your Moore Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on descaling once a year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters run 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models usually last 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing warning signs such as discolored hot water, rumbling during heating, dampness under the tank, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and much easier on you than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to high water pressure or a missing expansion tank, both inexpensive fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can wear out the valve and stress the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Common Water Heater Problems in Moore

Around Moore, water heater trouble tends to trace back to a handful of causes. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be a real factor: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

Age matters just as much. Many homes around here still have a tank that was installed a decade or more ago, and once a tank is past about ten years, the anode rod has usually been consumed and rust starts working on the steel from the inside. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from failed thermocouples and draft problems to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Code is another piece. Swapping out an older heater often means updating the installation along the way: an expansion tank if the water system is closed, a T&P discharge line that terminates correctly, venting that drafts properly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Well water brings its own challenges, including extra sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that occasionally develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these is fixable, and most cost far less when caught early. If your water heater is doing anything unusual, a quick diagnostic visit can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
